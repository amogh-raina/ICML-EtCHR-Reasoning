1. **Was there an interference with Article 10?**
   The applicants were prevented from standing in elections based on their alleged political activities and expression (participating in protests, social media activity, supporting opposition coalitions, etc.). Election candidacy is a form of political expression protected by Article 10 (see Lerma Blanquer v. Spain, no. 37949/18). The refusal to register them interfered with their right to freedom of expression.

2. **Was the interference prescribed by law?**
   Yes, the interference was based on the amended Electoral Rights Act (paragraph 11). However, we need to check if the law was accessible and foreseeable in its application (see Sunday Times v. UK, no. 28396/95).

3. **Legitimate aim?**
   The law aimed to prevent extremism, which could be considered a legitimate aim under Article 10(2) (national security, public safety, prevention of disorder or crime).

4. **Necessary in a democratic society?**
   This is the key test. The ECtHR has consistently held that restrictions on political expression must be subjected to the closest scrutiny (see Lingens v. Austria, no. 28881/07). 

   Important points from the facts:
   - The law was applied retroactively in effect - the organizations were declared extremist on June 9, but the law applied to elections after June 4, and the applicants' activities occurred before the declaration (see paragraphs 3-8)
   - The evidence relied on was police reports, not judicial decisions establishing individual involvement (paragraph 5, 9)
   - The activities in question (protests, social media support, signature collection) are core political expression
   - The law bars individuals for 3 years from the date of the organization's ban, regardless of when their involvement occurred (paragraph 11)

   Relevant case law:
   - In Sejdić and Finci v. Bosnia and Herzegovina (no. 25501/07), the Court found violations when electoral rights were restricted based on ethnic grounds
   - In Yerakshov v. Russia (no. 37920/07), the Court found issues with restrictions on passive voting rights
   - In Navalnyye v. Russia (no. 20261/12), the Court addressed restrictions on political rights

   The critical issue is whether the interference was proportionate. The ECtHR has been very protective of political speech (see Lingens, no. 28881/07). The fact that:
   - The restrictions were based on association with organizations that were only later declared extremist
   - The evidence didn't require individual judicial findings
   - The activities were peaceful political expression
   makes the interference likely disproportionate.

5. **Conclusion:**
   The interference with the applicants' Article 10 rights (through restricting their ability to stand for election based on their political expression) was not "necessary in a democratic society" because:
   - The law was applied in a way that lacked sufficient foresight (retroactive effect)
   - The evidence standard was too low (police reports without judicial determination)
   - The restriction affected core political expression
   - The duration (3 years) seems excessive given the nature of the activities
